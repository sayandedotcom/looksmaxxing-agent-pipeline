# Mewing and Your Jawline: What the Research Shows

You've read the threads. Half of them say mewing rebuilt someone's face in eighteen months, and the other half say it's cope. Here's what the literature supports, what it doesn't, and what you can do about it.

## The short version

We searched PubMed for "mewing," "tongue posture adult jaw shape change exercise," "tongue posture training adults mandible jawline randomized," and "tongue posture and craniofacial growth systematic review." We found no controlled study showing that mewing or deliberate tongue-posture training changes jaw or jawline shape in healthy adults.

That's not the same as a study proving it fails. Nobody has run the trial that would settle it, so the claim has no controlled evidence behind it. Treat anyone who sounds certain, in either direction, with some suspicion.

## Where the "tongue posture matters" idea comes from

The idea isn't pulled from nowhere. There's research linking tongue posture to jaw structure, but it's narrower than the forum version.

One study looked at people with Class III malocclusion and found that tongue posture was significantly lower in that group and was associated with the dentoalveolar characteristics of the maxilla and mandible. [PMID:22467568] The catch is that this was a cross-sectional association in a small sample. It shows two things tend to show up together. It doesn't show that changing your tongue posture changes your jaw. A lower tongue position could be a cause, a consequence, or just a bystander. A snapshot can't tell you which.

If you've ever argued "correlation isn't causation" in a thread and then ignored it when it favored your routine, this is that moment.

## The kid studies don't transfer to you

The more hopeful research is in growing children. A systematic review of early orthodontic and myofunctional treatment in kids called the approach promising but rated the quality of the existing evidence as questionable. [PMID:30152171] Those results come from children and don't extend to adults doing tongue posture at home. Growing kids and finished adults are different situations, and the review doesn't claim otherwise.

Another review found that in children, prefabricated myofunctional appliances were less effective than custom-made functional appliances at producing dental, skeletal or soft-tissue changes. [PMID:31597520] The evidence was moderate to low quality. The takeaway isn't that custom appliances are a magic fix. It's that even in the kid literature, the generic off-the-shelf version loses to the version fitted by a professional. Whatever you're doing with your tongue and a YouTube video is further down that ladder.

## Muscle exercises aren't bone exercises

Some people point to exercise research as proof that training the face works. Look at what was measured. A randomized study of orofacial muscle exercises after jaw surgery tracked muscle activity, not bone or jawline shape. The group differences in masseter and temporalis activity were gone by 180 days. [PMID:41468291]

So even the muscle outcome, which is the easier one to move, didn't hold a group difference at six months in that study. It tells you very little about whether tongue posture reshapes a jaw.

## Why the claims sound so convincing anyway

An analysis of online looksmaxxing content, including mewing and bonesmashing, found that posts use pseudoscientific language that lends them legitimacy. The authors say this helps normalize dangerous practices among young men. [PMID:42674114] Their analysis of the 100 most-engaged posts identified four dominant narratives: self-improvement as empowerment, pseudoscientific legitimacy, community belonging and mutual validation, and risk minimization and normalization. [PMID:42674114]

Notice how those fit together. The vocabulary sounds clinical, the community is supportive, and the risks get waved off. That's a good recipe for spreading a practice, and it says nothing about whether the practice works. The same applies to the self-improvement framing. Wanting to look better is fine. The question is whether the method has anything behind it.

## What to do instead

**Read before-and-after photos skeptically.** Angle, lighting, weight, and age all change how a jawline looks. A two-year "transformation" that includes a haircut, a cut in body fat, and a better camera isn't evidence about tongue posture.

**Don't expect mewing to replace surgery or orthodontics.** If a structural issue is what's bothering you, a tongue exercise isn't a substitute for treatment that's actually been studied for it.

**Get a real assessment if you have a real concern.** If you're worried about your jaw, bite, or breathing, see an orthodontist or an oral and maxillofacial surgeon for a proper cephalometric assessment. That gets you measurements instead of mirror guesses.

**If the goal is a sharper-looking jawline, ask a clinician about options** such as body fat reduction or professionally supervised cosmetic procedures. Those are conversations to have with someone qualified, not with a forum.

**Skip DIY bone-changing techniques like bonesmashing.** The research on online looksmaxxing flags this kind of practice as dangerous and notes how easily it gets normalized. There's no upside here worth finding out the hard way.

## Bottom line

Keeping your tongue against your palate is free and probably harmless, so if you like doing it, nobody's stopping you. Just don't confuse "free and probably harmless" with "proven to change your jaw." In adults, no controlled study we could find has tested that. The most relevant positive-sounding research is either an association in a small sample or work in children with questionable evidence quality.

If you want a different jaw, the path runs through measurements and a clinician. If you want a sharper-looking one, it may run through body fat and a conversation about supervised options. Neither one makes for a viral clip, but both are better bets than a six-month experiment on your own face.
